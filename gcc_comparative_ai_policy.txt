Comparing AI Policy Approaches Across the UAE and Saudi Arabia
====================================================================

Shared strategic logic
---------------------------
Both the UAE and Saudi Arabia treat artificial intelligence as a pillar of
post-oil economic diversification rather than as a narrow technology
portfolio. The UAE frames this through its National Strategy for Artificial
Intelligence 2031 and the wider Centennial 2071 vision; Saudi Arabia frames it
through the National Strategy for Data and AI under the umbrella of Vision
2030. Both countries set explicit, numeric economic targets rather than only
qualitative ambitions, and both created a dedicated ministerial or
authority-level role years before most peer governments did: the UAE
appointed the world's first Minister of State for Artificial Intelligence in
2017, while Saudi Arabia established SDAIA by royal decree in 2019.

Differences in institutional design
----------------------------------------
The two countries differ in how they distribute authority. The UAE spreads
responsibility across multiple bodies: the Minister of State for AI sets
policy direction, the Emirates Council for AI and Digital Transactions
coordinates federal and emirate-level execution, the UAE Data Office governs
personal data specifically, and the Cyber Security Council owns AI-specific
security policy. Saudi Arabia concentrates more authority inside a single
authority, SDAIA, which runs both data governance and AI development through
its NDMO and NCAI subsidiaries respectively. Neither structure is obviously
superior; the UAE's distributed model can move on parallel tracks but
requires more coordination, while Saudi Arabia's concentrated model
simplifies accountability but places more load on one institution.

Differences in commercial structure
-----------------------------------------
Saudi Arabia has more explicitly separated research from commercialisation,
with SDAIA and NCAI retaining research authority while HUMAIN, backed by the
Public Investment Fund, serves as the commercial and production arm for
advanced models such as ALLaM. The UAE's approach blends sovereign capital
and commercial development more directly through Mubadala and ADQ, which
both fund infrastructure and take direct stakes in AI companies, rather than
channelling that role through a single dedicated commercial entity.

Data protection as a shared foundation
-------------------------------------------
Both countries have moved to establish comprehensive personal data protection
laws as a foundation for trustworthy AI deployment: the UAE's Federal
Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 and Saudi Arabia's Personal Data Protection Law,
developed with SDAIA's involvement. Both laws draw on GDPR as a reference
point while adapting provisions to local legal and administrative structures,
and both explicitly anticipate that AI-driven automated decision-making
creates privacy and oversight obligations beyond ordinary data processing.

What this means for AI deployment in the region
-------------------------------------------------------
For an organisation or researcher operating across both markets, the
practical implication is that compliance cannot be treated as a single
regional standard: data residency rules, AI security expectations, and
risk-classification frameworks differ enough between the UAE and Saudi Arabia
that GDPR-style compliance, or compliance with one country's framework, does
not transfer automatically to the other. Both jurisdictions are also still in
active rule-making, with Saudi Arabia's AI risk framework still in draft form
and the UAE's AI Security Policy only issued in 2025, so organisations should
expect continued change rather than a settled baseline.
